# You're the buyer: thanks for helping! 🙏

You don't need to know anything about sales or tech. Read this page once (about 5 minutes) and keep it open during the call. You can read your lines straight off the page.

---

## What's going on (the big picture)

Your friend is practising for a **sales job**. Salespeople practise on fake calls with a friend playing a customer. That's you.

- **Your friend plays** a salesperson from **AssemblyAI**, a real company that makes software that **listens to audio and turns speech into written text**. Think of the auto-captions on YouTube, but for businesses.
- **You play** a busy boss at a company that might buy that software.
- **The call is recorded.** Afterwards your friend runs the recording through a program that grades their performance: how much they talked, whether they asked good questions, and whether they got a next meeting.

**Your job is to be realistic, not nice.** Real bosses are busy and don't hand out information for free. If you make it too easy, your friend doesn't learn anything.

---

## Who you are

**Name:** Dana Reyes
**Job title:** COO (Chief Operating Officer). You're the person who makes sure the business runs smoothly day to day.
**Company:** Lumora (made up), about 80 employees

### What your company does (plain English)
Big insurance companies have call centers where staff answer customer calls all day ("I want to make a claim", "what does my policy cover?"). Those calls are recorded.

**Lumora's software listens to those recordings and turns them into written text.** Then it writes a short summary of each call, so insurance managers can quickly see what happened, spot unhappy customers and check their staff said the right things.

You process a lot of calls: **about 40,000 hours of recordings every month.**

### How the text gets made today
You use a **free tool called Whisper** to turn speech into text. It's free to download, but your company has to run it on its own computers and keep it working. That's the catch.

### Your story (why this matters to you)
- **The problem:** Whisper keeps getting **insurance words and policy numbers wrong.** For example, it writes "deductible" as "deduct a bull", or gets a policy number like "PX-4471" wrong. So the summaries are wrong too, and customers complain.
- **It already cost you:** last quarter a customer **left partly because of this.** They were paying about **$200,000 a year.** Your CEO was not happy.
- **It's eating your team's time:** **two engineers spend half their time** fixing and babysitting Whisper instead of building new features.
- **The deadline:** your **biggest customer's contract renews in two months.** If accuracy doesn't improve, you could lose them too. This keeps you up at night, but you don't tell strangers that easily.

### Who else is involved
- **You** make the final decision on spending money.
- **Sam**, your tech lead (VP of Engineering), has to agree the new tool actually works before you'd switch.

### Why you agreed to this call
A colleague forwarded the salesperson's email and said "worth 5 minutes?" You said yes to be polite. You're **not** expecting much, and you're between two meetings.

### Your mood
Polite but impatient. You glance at the clock. You respect people who get to the point.

## Before you start
1. **Set a 5-minute timer** on your phone, where your friend can't see it.
2. Your friend starts recording and **says hello first.**
3. You reply with your first line.

### Your first line
> "Hi, I've only got five minutes. What's this about?"

---

## Your secrets 🤫

**Only share a secret if they ask about it.** If they don't ask, don't tell. That's the whole game: you're testing whether they ask the right questions.

| If they ask about... | Example questions | You say |
|---|---|---|
| **Problems** | "What's not working?" "Any challenges with your current setup?" | "Honestly, our transcripts get insurance terms and policy numbers wrong. Customers complain the summaries aren't reliable." |
| **How bad it is** | "What does that cost you?" "What happens when it's wrong?" | "We lost a customer last quarter, partly over this. About $200,000 a year." |
| **What you use now** | "What are you using today?" "How do you do it now?" | "We run a free tool called Whisper ourselves. But two of my engineers spend half their time keeping it working." |
| **Who decides** | "Who else would be involved?" "How do you make decisions like this?" | "I sign off, but my tech lead Sam has to agree it actually works." |
| **Timing** | "Is there a deadline?" "Why now?" | "Our biggest customer's contract renews in two months. I need this fixed before then." |
| **Money** | "What's your budget?" "How would you think about cost?" | "If it costs less than two half-time engineers, I'm listening." |

**Good questions earn more detail.** If they ask a sharp follow-up, like "what did losing that customer mean for you?", you can add a bit more feeling: "It was a bad quarter. My CEO asked me to fix it."

---

## How to react to what they do

| If your friend... | You do this |
|---|---|
| Talks for a long time about their product without asking you anything (about 20 seconds or more) | Interrupt: **"Sorry, I really don't have time for a product tour."** |
| Asks a vague question ("How's business?" "How's everything going?") | Short, boring answer: **"Fine."** or **"It works."** |
| Asks a specific question about your business | Answer from the secrets table |
| Uses lots of tech jargon | **"I'm not technical. What does that mean for my business?"** |
| Says something like "We're the most accurate" without proof | **"Everyone says that."** |
| Respects your time ("I'll keep it quick, can I ask two questions?") | Relax a little: **"Sure, go ahead."** |

### Push back once
At some point in the middle of the call, say:
> **"Why would I pay for this when Whisper is free?"**

- If they **ask you a question back** (like "What does it cost you to keep it running?"), soften: "Fair point. It's not really free when you count my engineers."
- If they just **argue or list features**, stay unconvinced: "Hmm. I'm not sure."

---

## How to end the call

- **They asked for a specific next step** (a meeting with a day or time, or a meeting with Sam):
  > "Okay, that's fair. Set something up with Sam for next week."
- **They didn't ask, or only said "I'll send you some info":**
  > "Sure, just send me an email."
- **The timer went off:**
  > "Sorry, I have to jump into my next meeting."
  Then end the call, even mid-sentence. Busy bosses really do this.

---

## What if...

**They ask something not on this page?**
Make up an answer that fits Dana's story. Keep it short. Nobody will check.

**They ask a technical question I don't understand?**
Say "I'd have to ask Sam about that." That's exactly what a real COO would say.

**I forget a secret or say it wrong?**
No problem. Getting it roughly right is fine.

**I laugh or break character?**
Totally fine. Take a breath and keep going.

---

## After the call
Tell your friend honestly:
1. **One thing they did well.**
2. **One moment you'd have stopped listening** if this were real.
3. **Would you actually take the next meeting?** Yes or no, and why.

That honest feedback helps them more than anything else. Thanks for doing this 😄
